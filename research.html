Talking over a jammer

Two radios, one very loud interferer on the same frequency, and a receiver that still gets the message. My PhD, with Nikos Sidiropoulos at UVA, is about how. Everything below runs live in your browser.

The idea in five steps

  1. 1. PrecodeThe transmitter spreads each packet over a few extra dimensions with a linear precoder, leaving those directions unused.
  2. 2. CollideAn interferer, possibly hostile, lands on top of it.
  3. 3. ListenThe receiver has two antennas and hears two different mixtures.
  4. 4. AlignIt looks for the mix that is silent in the unused directions. Only a mix with no interference can be.
  5. 5. DecodeWhat is left is your packet. One known pilot symbol fixes the remaining scale and phase.

Data rate with extra symbols

A simplified model: 16-symbol QPSK packets, two receive antennas, one Gaussian interferer that does not know the precoder, and a fresh random channel every packet. One symbol per packet is a known pilot, and the data rate counts it as overhead. The real system adds pulse shaping, synchronization and frequency-offset correction, and was tested over the air.

Why it works

Your packet is the only thing in two places at once

Precoding leaves a few directions unused. The packet never touches them and the interference does. Two antennas make everything the receiver hears lie in a two-dimensional space, and that space overlaps the set of valid packets in exactly one line: the packet.

The earlier version of this idea, from our group, repeated every packet to get the second view and so halved the rate. A linear precoder gets the same overlap for the price of a few extra slots.

Finding the packet

The receiver has to know where to look

A receiver doesn't know when a packet starts. Slide a window along the stream and ask how well each position lines up. Only the true packet boundary can cancel the unused directions, so it stands out. It needs enough interference to give the receiver something to cancel: turn the interference down and the peak fades. This is the idea behind my fast timing-acquisition method; the full version cheaply screens the offsets before doing the expensive work, and its identifiability argument is machine-checked in Lean 4.

Real hardware

Not just a simulation

I built the whole thing on software-defined radios: transmitter, receiver, pulse shaping, synchronization, carrier frequency offset correction. Measured in a lab where signal and interference power are known, then as an underlay transmission under a commercial Wi-Fi access point on 2.4 GHz. The code is a Rust library with Python bindings, plus a toolchain that transmits, captures IQ, decodes and reports error rates.
